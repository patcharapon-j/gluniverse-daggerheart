// AN AGREEMENT NOBODY CAN SEE IS FOUR AGREEMENTSFiction-first is not the same as unmeasured

Daggerheart's ranges are agreements at the table and the book is explicit about it — the feet printed beside Melee, Very Close, Close, Far and Very Far are its own approximations rather than a conversion table. That is the reason to draw them, not a reason not to: an approximation nobody can see is not one agreement, it is four people holding four of them, and the disagreement surfaces after somebody has already committed to a move.

The board is a stand-in for Foundry's own: a grid at 100px to the square with a heavier rule every five, three other creatures to show what a large ruler costs when it crosses somebody else's painting, and a camera that pans as well as zooms — this component is measured in thousands of scene pixels and the token chip's board never had to go that far out.

At the usual five feet to the square. The radius is measured from the token's edge, not its centre, because reach is what the rule means — a three-by-three dragon threatens a square beyond its own body, and measuring from its middle would put half of Melee inside the dragon. // THE ONE THAT DOES NOT FITVery Far is the whole scene

Very Far is 120 feet, which is twenty-four squares — a ring 4,900 pixels across before the token is added. A default Foundry scene is four thousand by three thousand. Set the bands to 5 above and the outermost ring is not a measurement, it is a claim that the answer is everywhere, and it drags the whole ladder out with it: at the zoom where Very Far fits on screen, Melee is nine pixels across and has already left. Four bands is the recommendation, and the fifth is declined out loud rather than quietly omitted.

// CERTAINTY IS THE LINE STYLEThe line says how much the line means

A surveyor's convention doing real work here. Melee and Very Close are solid, because "within arm's reach" is a thing you can be sure of. Close and Far break into dashes, and the dash is an arc length rather than an angle — a fixed angular period would make Far's dashes eight times longer than Melee's and the four rings would stop reading as one instrument.

The ruler has no hue, and that is a rule rather than a gap. Every colour on a token in this system already means a resource: wound is Hit Points, strain is Stress, plate is Armor, gold is Hope, violet is Fear. A ruler measures the ground rather than the creature, so it is drawn in the map's own ink and buys its legibility with contrast and contour — the same dark drop-shadow contour the chip's arcs use, and for the same reason.

A band is four layers and each is a claim rather than a different opacity of one, which is token.css's rule about the chip's arcs arriving somewhere with more room for it. The bevel is light bleeding inward off the line and shadow falling outward — one claim about one edge, so one gradient — and it is what makes the ring sit in the ground instead of on it. The line carries the certainty. A finer companion rule just outside it is how this design draws a border everywhere else, and it is what stops a single stroke reading as a fence. Then the lettering. None of it is a fill: a wash over the disc would tint the map.

The light on the line is a second mask, not an overlay, and that is not a preference. A conic alpha intersected with the annulus dims the ring toward the lower right, so it reads as a solid thing lit from above; an overlay in plus-lighter doing the same job would brighten the gaps in a dashed ring as readily as the dashes, and there would be no dash left. Composited, the light can only ever take away — the same guarantee the chip's facet ring gives its numerals.

// THE NAME FOLLOWS THE RINGTwo arcs, because one puts a third of every ring upside down

The name is on the band and repeats around it, which is what makes a ring identifiable from wherever you happen to be looking at the board rather than one you have to trace back to a tag somewhere on it. It is the chip's own gesture — token.css bends VULNERABLE round a circle for exactly this reason, that a token has no room for a caption and every room for a word.

Two half-arcs rather than one circle, and that is the whole of the readability. A single circular path puts the bottom third of every ring upside down; an upper arc running left-to-right over the top and a lower arc running left-to-right under the bottom are both upright. It is the map-maker's answer to labelling a contour and it costs one extra path. paint-order is the other half: stroking a dark halo first and filling over it knocks a channel out of the line behind every glyph, so the words read as cut into the ring rather than laid on top of it — which is also why this needs no plinth. A box would be an object on the map, and the map is what you came to look at.

// PLACED, NOT POUREDThe chip's word ring does not survive being twelve squares across

The chip fills its circle exactly, with textLength forcing the repeat to the path's own length so no gap travels round with the words. Ask that of Far and it is about ninety repeats and a thousand glyphs; cap the repeat while still forcing the length and eleven letters spread over half a circle. Those are the same mechanism failing from either end.

So the legends are placed — N per half arc, evenly spaced, each at its natural width, with the count taken from the arc's length on screen. Drag the zoom: a ring gains more legends as the camera comes in rather than larger ones, and it is rewritten only when that count actually changes, which is setTier's discipline rather than a similar one. The offsets are (m + ½)/N, a fence-post rule doing real work — it keeps every run clear of the two seams at three and nine o'clock, where the arcs meet and a run anchored to the end of one would hang off it.

// THE RINGS SCALE AND THE TYPE DOES NOTA legend that shrinks with the map is a legend twice

A ring is a distance on the ground, so it is drawn in scene pixels and rides the camera exactly as the grid does. The lettering is chrome about a ring, so it counter-scales and holds one size on screen at every zoom. Run the zoom slider from end to end: the circles breathe with the map and the words never change size. Below the ladder's floor a ring is not a small ring — it is a dot inside the creature, its stroke wider than the gap to its neighbour — so it leaves, which is the chip's own ladder arriving at a component that measures the ground.

The lettering needs a ladder of its own and it is a different question. Two bands a hundred scene pixels apart stay a hundred pixels apart on the ground forever; on screen that gap shrinks with the camera while the type does not, so below some zoom Melee's words print through Very Close's however far apart the circles are. Walked from the outside in, which is the half that matters: the outermost band is the one still worth reading at the zoom where the type is crowding, and keeping the innermost instead would answer a pulled-back camera with the word MELEE. Take the zoom down to about 0.2 and watch Melee's name go while its ring stays.

// NO IDLE MOTIONEverything here is an arrival or a departure

The ban in gem.css has taken two exceptions and both earned it the same way: the Fear strip and Vulnerable are each a live threat sitting in the room between the moments anybody touches them. A ruler is not that. It is a measurement you take, read, and stop looking at — and a ring turning slowly under a token somebody is trying to move is motion competing with the gesture it exists to serve. So the rings arrive staggered outward at 62ms, one wave leaves the creature and is gone, and after that nothing on this layer moves until the selection does.

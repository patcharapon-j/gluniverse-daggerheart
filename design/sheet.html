// THE MARKSThree tracks, three materials

The mark is a filled shape now, not a stroke. A stroked X is one width from end to end, which is what a printer does and not what a pen, a blade or a hammer does — every real mark is thin where it enters, widest where the force was, and thin again where it leaves. So each arm is a spindle running corner to corner — a mark that stops short of the edge is a decoration sitting inside a box; a mark that crosses it is the box crossed out — and the profile is where each track's character lives before any colour is applied. Hit Points get a seventeen-point tear whose centreline bows off the diagonal, because flesh does not part in a line. Stress gets a hairline scratch, dead straight and doubled, because something dragged under load rarely leaves one line. Armor gets a chisel: four points, straight edges, twice the weight of the other two.

Two things had to be wrong first. Width: at a quarter of their own length these were not strokes at all — two spindles that fat cross in a lozenge as big as either of them, and the eye resolves the whole thing as one four-pointed star. A pen stroke is under a tenth of its length. And the core, which is the inner shading: scaled about the box centre, a 0.46 copy of a spindle is the middle third of it — precisely the patch where the two arms already overlap — so it lands on the crossing and puts a hole in the star. It is narrowed across the arm instead, and offset rather than centred: a centred inner shape reads as a symmetric facet, which is what kept both the wound and the plate looking like folded paper. Shifted, it becomes an edge — a wet highlight along the lip of the tear, a fuller down the middle of the blade.

Damage and Stress share a box size. Seven and six divided independently is two different box sizes stacked in one column, and two sizes read as two kinds of thing. They are the same box, so both rows are sized for the larger count and the shorter one just ends early. The damage band's cost cells changed for the same reason: they were little grey squares, which said the right thing in the wrong vocabulary now that the boxes underneath are red marks. They are the same mark at tick size and in the same red, so the band says "this zone costs two of those" by drawing two of those.

The box no longer moves. It used to compress and overshoot on every mark, which is the gem's grammar borrowed wholesale — and wrong here, because Hope arrives one at a time and Hit Points arrive three at a time. Seven boxes bouncing is a row of jelly. Everything the box has to say it now says without a transform: the recess flushes with the mark's colour and the ring flashes, and it is still. All the motion belongs to the mark.

And the three arrivals are three different events. A wound lands whole and fast — you do not watch a cut being drawn — and then bleeds, the glow welling up over 340ms, four times longer than the strike itself; two arms, staggered, because it tore twice. Stress is scraped in along its own axis under load, and the recess flickers twice rather than flushing once, because strain is something giving, catching and giving again. Armor is a blow: both arms land together, one impact, with a gold flash and a hard square ring — and the box's corners are broken away where the mark exits, filled with the sheet's own colour so the corner is gone rather than shaded, with the fracture edge stroked back in and two hairline cracks drawing inward after. That break is the reason the clip-path moved off the box and onto a child: a clipped element clips its descendants, so a box that clipped itself could never be broken by anything.

Stress is cold on purpose. Gold is Hope, violet is Fear, red is a wound — a cyan scratch is the quadrant of the wheel that is left, and it is the reason a full Stress row turning violet is unmissable. A track whose resting colour was already Fear's could not turn into it. This is also the one place the system's "hue means domain" rule is suspended, and it is worth suspending: these are the three things you mark under pressure, and the cost of confusing them is a wrong decision at the table. They are told apart by substance first and hue second, so the distinction survives a screenshot and the light theme both.

// THE ATTACK BARThe only control on the sheet

Everything else here is a record you edit. This is a button you press, and it does the two sums players actually get wrong. Attack is 2d12 + trait plus whatever the weapon's feature adds — the Broadsword's Reliable is a number the sheet applies, not a sentence someone has to remember. Damage is Proficiency copies of the die, not one die; this is the single most-missed rule in the game and the sheet already knows your Proficiency, so there is no excuse for making anyone multiply it themselves. The two dice on the button are the system's own diamonds because they are the system's own diamonds; damage dice are chamfered squares instead, so the silhouette tells you which roll you are looking at before you have read a number. Rolling with Hope grants you one, and a critical grants a Hope and clears a Stress — watch the rail.

The dice tumble on a timer, not on requestAnimationFrame, and that is the same bug as settle.js's wearing a different hat: rAF does not fire in a window that is not painting, so the first version left rolling latched the moment the sheet was occluded — grey dice and a placeholder total on a roll that had already been decided. A timer is throttled in the background rather than stopped, so the tumble degrades to a couple of steps and the result still lands, which is the right way round. The result was never at stake either way: it is decided before any of this runs and written into the markup first, and the tumble only ever overwrites the display.

// EQUIPMENTTwo hands, and a rule about them

Three slots, drawn as three slots, with everything else you own listed underneath — click a carried item to equip it. The Secondary slot is where the rules get interesting: you cannot hold one if your primary needs both hands, so with the Wyrmwood Longbow equipped the slot is blocked and says why, in violet, because a blocked slot is a rule acting on you rather than a choice you have not made yet. Equip the Broadsword and the Round Shield becomes available; equip the shield and the Armor Score in the rail goes to 5 while the Armor Slots stay at 4 — those were one field and are now two, because they are two numbers and a shield only raises one of them.

// THE SHEETRail and pane

The class tab is gone. A character has exactly one ancestry, one community, one class and between one and three subclass cards — its contents could never change, and a tab whose contents never change is a heading that costs a click. Those four are rows in the main tab now, last, where they cost almost nothing: the tab is ordered by how often each thing is reached for, the same rule the rail follows. The attack bar is touched every round, the loadout every few, and the class and heritage cards are read carefully once and then almost never — which is why they are spines with the card on hover, the same row the domain loadout uses, rather than four fixed cards at full size. Four permanent paragraphs at the bottom of the tab you open most is a lot of sheet to spend on text nobody re-reads, and it means every card here is now reached the same way.

Dark is the base theme, and it is a substrate change rather than a repaint. Every hex that assumed a white sheet became a role — --paper, --sunk, --tint, --sev-1..4, and now the three mark palettes. The damage band is the interesting one: severity is expressed as weight, so the ramp runs the other way in the dark and Severe becomes the near-white zone. Toggle light above; a wound has to be darker than white paper to read as a hole in it and brighter than a dark sheet, so those values differ by more than lightness.

Everything is live. Mark Hit Points, Stress, Armor Slots and advancement slots by clicking a box; click a coin to count it; roll attacks and damage; equip and unequip gear. Hover any row — loadout, vault, class, subclass, ancestry, community — for the full card — those render into a layer that is a child of the sheet body rather than of the tab pane, because a pane that scrolls clips its own children and no z-index fixes that.

Both animation drivers were leaking their state classes, and one of the leaks was not cosmetic. Animation.finished looks like the right completion signal and does not settle in this engine — a CSS animation reaches playState:"finished" with the promise still pending, verified against a two-line control animation on a bare div. So the cleanup never ran: every marked box kept hit, and every spent Hope gem kept on spend, which means a spent Hope stayed lit. It failed in the direction that looks like nothing is wrong, which is how it survived a round of verification that only checked the animations fired. animationend was the obvious replacement and is only half an answer — it needs frames, and an occluded window advances the timeline without producing any. So settle.js races the event count against a deadline it reads back off getComputedTiming().endTime: not a magic number, and still correct when a duration changes in the CSS.

// EDIT MODEDefinition against use

Every derived number on this sheet hangs off something you set once. Level drives the tier, Proficiency, both damage thresholds and what every advancement panel owes you; the class hands over base Evasion, Hit Points and Stress; a trait score is chosen at creation and moved perhaps twice in a whole campaign. During play you are doing the other thing entirely — marking, spending, clearing, equipping, taking — dozens of times an hour, under a clock, with a GM waiting. A stray click on Level or a trait in the middle of that moves a number nobody notices moved until a sum is wrong three sessions later. So the sheet has two states, and the split is definition against use: everything you do stays live in both, and everything that says what this character is is unlocked by the mode.

It is not the adjust tab, and the two must not be folded together. That tab overrides numbers the rules derive, which is adjudication and therefore the GM's — it is gated on isGM. This unlocks a character's own definition, which is authorship and therefore the owner's — it is gated on being able to edit the sheet at all, because a player levelling up needs it and has no business on the dials. A GM playing their own character is exactly the case where one is true and the other should not be. The adjust tab now answers both questions, which is why its fields go inert with everything else.

The toggle sits at the end of the tab strip, not in the window's title bar. That bar is the application — its close button, its drag handle — and this is a state of the sheet, one of the two it can be in. It goes after the counter rather than beside the tabs so it is not read as a seventh one, and it takes none of a tab's vocabulary: no gold top rule, no paper fill. What it takes instead is a box — the same chamfered pip an advancement slot is made of, filling with strain when the mode is on, which is this sheet's own shape for a state that is either set or not. It is position:sticky at the right edge, because the strip scrolls horizontally at six tabs in a narrow window and a control you have to go looking for is a control that is not there.

The banner takes height rather than overlaying, and that is the one real trade. .win.dropping overlays — a gold edge, pointer-events:none, nothing moves — and that is right for a drag, which lasts a second and during which a reflow under a held pointer would make you re-aim mid-gesture. This lasts as long as you are editing, and the two failures are not symmetrical: an overlay that persists is a strip sitting permanently on top of the diorama and the gold rows, covering the very controls the mode exists to unlock, and pointer-events:none does not give the pixels back. The band costs one reflow, on the frame you pressed the toggle — a deliberate gesture whose answer is the sheet changing shape — and it costs nothing after: .bd is flex:1 1 auto inside a fixed window, so the 36px comes off the body and the two scrollers absorb it. Nothing moves sideways and no panel changes width. Strain, not gold: gold is Hope's currency everywhere in this system — the level plate, the gems, the coins, the spellcast spark — and a second meaning for it is a second thing to decode. It is two identical runs inside one track translated by exactly half the track's width, so the loop has no seam, and it is pure CSS; a JS ticker would be a requestAnimationFrame running for as long as somebody leaves a sheet open, to move some text. Under prefers-reduced-motion the track parks at its own start and the band simply sits there, still legible.

Locked is a state, not an absence. A field that vanishes when you flip a switch makes the switch feel destructive — the adjust tab learned that already with .off — and a value you can no longer read because it is locked is worse than one you can read and cannot type into. So .def keeps its ink and gives up its recess: the recess is the part that said "type here", and without it the number is a printed figure on the panel, which is what it is. The level plate and the trait numeral do not even do that much — b and input.b have been drawn as one declaration since the plate was written, so those two swap between a caret and no caret and nothing else moves. Every panel the mode locks says so in a sentence underneath.

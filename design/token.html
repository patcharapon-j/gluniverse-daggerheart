// TWO DRAFTS THROWN AWAYThe empty box was doing all the damage

The first build ported the sheet's row unchanged: twelve Hit Points, six Stress, three Armor Slots and six Hope — twenty-seven objects at seven pixels on a 120px token. That is texture, not a readout, and the culprit was the box that is empty: on the sheet an empty box is the affordance, the thing you press, and nothing on a token is pressable. It carried no information the row's own length did not already carry and cost exactly as much ink as a mark. Half the component was drawing the part of the sheet that only existed to be clicked.

The second took the empty boxes out and still lost, on two counts. A centred row that fills from the left puts 5-of-12 visibly off-centre with a bare rule trailing right. And it sat on the artwork anyway — the circle is inscribed in the square, so the bottom band at centre is the painting, and only the corners are free.

// THE GAUGEThe circumference is three times the width

Every track shares one origin and one direction: they start at 210°, lower-left, and run clockwise up over the top to lower-right. The 60° left open at six o'clock is not waste — it is the slot where Hope sits on a character and Difficulty on an adversary. The rings open for the thing the creature spends.

Radially, outermost first: Armor, then Hit Points, then Stress. That order is the rule rather than a layout — armour is what stands between a hit and your Hit Points, so it stands outside them.

And all three are outside the sprite. The innermost track's inner edge is at radius 50.2, which is the first tenth of a pixel past the token's own circle: the creature is drawn and the tracks are drawn, and neither is drawn over the other. Every earlier draft put them on the painting, which is an argument a 3px arc loses against a pale highlight however saturated it is. Conditions are the exception: their procedural material stays on the creature while their joined sentence sits almost flush to the rim. They are not tracks and never become another countable band. The cost is a footprint: the outer edge is at 64.6 where the grid cell's is 50, so a chip reaches a seventh of a cell into each neighbour and two adjacent creatures' Armor arcs can cross. That is what buys the artwork back.

// ARMOR IS NOT A RINGThe arc's length is the capacity

Armor is two or three slots on most characters and can be six, and a circle divided into two is not a track, it is a pie chart. So Armor alone runs at a fixed angular pitch and stops when it runs out — three slots is 51° of arc, six is 102° — so the arc's own length is the capacity. A full ring normalises exactly that away, which is the one fact about armour anybody wants at a glance.

The pitch was 13° and the band was the narrowest of the three at 3.0px, and both had it backwards. Armor is the outermost track, so it is the only one drawn against the map rather than against another arc of ours; it is the only one that lights what is left, so a nearly-spent purse is a nearly-empty ring with nothing to hold the eye; and its slots are the shortest marks on the chip, because it alone stops. Three claims all pointing the same way, and the answer to every one of them is ink — 17° and 4.2px, the widest band rather than the narrowest.

It also reads the other way round, and it is the only track that does. Hit Points and Stress are things that happen to you: the mark is the damage, the lit run grows as the fight goes badly, and an empty ring is a creature that is fine. Armor is not damage — it is a purse, slots you still have to spend, which is the number anybody actually asks at the table and the number the damage dialog counts down while you decide. So Armor lights what is left and goes dark as it is spent. Drawn like the damage tracks it would put a bright band meaning nothing on a fresh character and nothing at all on a spent one, which is exactly backwards.

// HOPE CURVESNot a caption under a gauge

Hope is gold diamonds everywhere else in this system and a token is not where that gets re-taught, so it is gem.js's own GEM. What changes is the placement: each gem is put down by angle, on the tracks' own circle, and tilts with it — filling the 60° the rings leave open at six o'clock. A straight row underneath would be a caption below a gauge, and Hope is not a caption; it is the fourth thing the creature carries. The row is centred on six o'clock and takes only the arc it needs, exactly as Armor takes only the arc its slots need.

The gems rotate rather than counter-rotate, which is deliberate: a diamond is symmetric about both axes, so a tilt costs it no legibility at all, while a row of upright gems on a curve reads as a row that has been bent — the one thing it must not look like.

// MATERIALThree layers, three claims, not three opacities

.ch is the channel — every slot the track has, recessed. It is what says "fourteen" while only six are marked, and it is what lets an unmarked slot be genuinely absent from .lit rather than a washed-out copy of a marked one. .lit is what is marked, and carries the glow. .fx is the landing, faded out on its own — which is how a conic gradient gets setMarks' arrival back with no element per slot.

Substance is what the ring costs, and this is how much comes back. Hue and radius do most of the telling apart, then Stress is scored — fine hairlines across the annulus at a frequency nothing else here has, the scratch's own character — and Armor is plate, the brightest of the three and the only one with a specular run down its outer edge. It is not the tear, the scratch and the chisel. It is a good deal more than three colours.

There used to be a fourth layer and it is gone: a bevel on the channel, a pale ramp at each track's outer edge. On the two inner tracks it was invisible under the lit run. On Armor, which is the outermost band and therefore the place the chip meets the map, it drew a continuous pale ring all the way round the chip whether anything was marked or not — light with no object under it, and the first thing anybody noticed about the component. The specular survives it and is the same technique, because it rides the lit run: it appears only where a slot is, and a highlight along the top of a filled slot is exactly what plate does.

// THE RINGThe cell is not where the creature ends

Every radius on this chip is written against one assumption — that the creature ends at the grid cell's own circle, radius 50 — and three arrangements a table can be in break it: a dynamic token ring in either of its fit modes, and a token whose artwork is scaled. When it breaks the chip is drawn on the painting, which is the one thing this component promised not to do.

The numbers are not measured and not guessed. Foundry publishes the ring texture's own proportions in the spritesheet beside the artwork — startRadius 0.666, the edge of the subject's hole, and endRadius 0.7225, where the visible band stops. The two fit modes are two normalisations of that one texture, which is why one constant answers both and they are reciprocals: fit the hole to the cell and the rim lands 1.0848 cells out, fit the rim to the cell and the hole stops at 0.9218 of it. The first is the collision. The second is the one arrangement where the creature is smaller than its cell and the condition sentence still follows the visible rim.

The pale annulus is Foundry's band at its own two radii. Press before to put the chip back where it was drawn: in subject fit the innermost track begins at 50.2 and the band's rim reaches 54.2, so the two are on top of each other.

multiplier 1.00 · the table's own, and it moves every chip on the board

Radii only. A band's width, a gem and a numeral keep their size at every setting. That is the range ruler's rule arriving here — the rings are geometry and scale with what they measure, the lettering is read and does not — and it is what stops the dial making one creature's Stress track fatter than its neighbour's. The readout is floored at the cell however small the art is: following a 0.6-scale sprite inward would draw its track at 0.6 the size of the creature beside it, and being countable across a whole fight at a glance is the entire job. The procedural condition material needs no radius calculation: it is a filter on the PIXI mesh and follows the creature by construction.

// CONDITIONSOne sentence outside, one material inside

A condition is not a fourth track. Its recognition text sits almost flush to the token rim while a procedural material changes the portrait itself. The effect is circular, clipped by the mesh, and never sends particles or ornaments beyond the token.

Multiple conditions are composited before the portrait is tinted: red and blue make a saturated purple material instead of two translucent layers bleaching the face. Their names join the same sentence — ABLAZE · VULNERABLE · — rather than becoming competing text rings.

Sixteen pattern families carry different motion, deformation and texture signatures. Reduced motion freezes time without removing the material. Defeated is terminal: it suppresses all live conditions and resources, freezes the shader, and separates the portrait into seven physical fragments with true void between them. There is no death icon.

And textLength is what makes the word ring seamless. A repeated string almost never comes out to the exact circumference, and the leftover shows as a gap travelling round with the text; forcing the run to the path's own length with lengthAdjust="spacing" closes it without touching a glyph.

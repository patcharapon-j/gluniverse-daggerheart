// THE DIEThe solid, not one face of it

It started as a single pentagon — the face a d12 shows you when it lands. It is the whole solid now, seen down a face normal: decagon outline, a pentagon inside it at 0.618 of the radius for the front face, and five facets between them. That is the shape a player already has in their hand, and it is why a d12 and a d20 are told apart across a table long before anyone counts anything — a dodecahedron is round and a d20 is angular.

Both are the true projection and both land on the same number. Down a 5-fold axis a dodecahedron's front-face vertices project to radius 1.0514 and its silhouette vertices to 1.7013; down a 3-fold axis an icosahedron's to 1.1547 and 1.8683. Both ratios are 0.618 — φ−1, the golden section that builds both solids. So the d20 is a hexagon with an upward triangle at 0.618R, and the d12 is a decagon with a pentagon at 0.618R.

The d12 was drawn as a pentagon for a while, on the argument that a decagon at 38px is a coin. It was — but the fault was the shading, not the silhouette. With a flat ring and no lit edges any ten-sided outline is a disc. Faceted properly it reads as the solid, and the silhouette differing from the front face is exactly what makes it read as a solid rather than as one face seen straight on.

It keeps the gem construction — flat saturated front face, a brighter rim where the light leaves the edge, a bloom on a wrapper so the filter is not clipped away by the shape it describes — and it keeps Hope's gold and Fear's violet. What it gives up is the diamond, and that is the right trade: the diamond stays on the sheet, where it is the pip you mark. The losing die is still unlit rather than greyed. The advantage d6 is a square, because that is a d6’s face and there is nothing folded back to shade — see rolls for what it does and how several of them behave when allies Help.

The facets are told apart by value first — a conic with hard stops on the facet boundaries — and then by a lit edge at each boundary, which is what rescued the decagon. That edge is not a hairline: a 1px stroke at these sizes is a sub-pixel line at partial alpha, the same grey smudge the X ran into, so it is a 5° wedge — 2px at the outer rim, 1.2px at the inner one, never below the rendering floor at any radius. It is painted in exactly the face colour, because a real edge catches the light.

And the ring only ever darkens: the front face is square to you and catches the most light. That is correct lighting and it is also the guarantee — no numeral ever sits on anything lighter than the face colour it was measured against, so every contrast figure on this sheet survives the facets. Sector values are cos of the angle to a light at the upper left, mapped to 6–26% black: the floor is 6 so the front face's outline never vanishes into the facet beside it on the lit side, and the ceiling is 26 because that is where the gold d12's dark numeral still measures 4.8:1 on its deepest facet.

The numeral is bigger — .66 of the die, up from .52, with every shape's step-down rising in the same proportion so the set still reads as one set — and thickened with a stroke, because Google Sans stops at 700 and 700 on a lit gold face is thinner than the face deserves. The damage plate draws its dice at 26px and there the dice are the subject: at .52 the digits stood 9.9px of cap height inside a 26px chip, which is a picture of a die with a caption on it; at .66 they stand 12.6px, half the chip, and the number is the thing you read first. They overhang the front face onto the facets on every shape, the way 20 always has on the d20: the numeral is painted on the solid, not fitted inside one plane of it, and that survives because the ring may only ever darken.

The old rule was nothing overflows the die's own box, and it was buying a ceiling nobody had asked for. None of the three candidates is really the limit. The clip is .pl, the only overflow:hidden ancestor, and the strip sits 12px inside it. The neighbour is a 5px gap, and two digits at .66 measure 20.2px inside a 26px box, so on a square the ink never reaches the box edge at all — it would take .85 of the die to get there. The silhouette is the limit, and it binds on the d10: 10 at .61 fills the kite out to its shoulders, and one step further puts the 1 and the 0 outside the outline, at which point it stops reading as a number painted on a solid and starts reading as a number in front of one.

Every numeral is centred on the silhouette — one rule, six shapes, no exceptions. It used to ride the front face's area centroid instead, on the reasoning that the numeral is painted on the one plane square to you: 66.67% down on the d4, where a real d4 prints it, 36.67% on the d8, (891·33 + 864·54.67)/1755 = 43.67% on the d10's kite. True of the object, and unreadable as a picture — the front face is a slightly lighter region on a 26px chip, so the outline is the only geometry a reader actually has, and against the outline those three had a number sitting off-centre for a reason nothing on screen could show. The d8 broke first and loudest, its ink standing 45% tall on a 40% face; the d10 states the principle best, a pixel and a half off centre, too small to read as projection and big enough to read as a mistake.

What is left is a real correction rather than a placement: Google Sans has ascent .965 and descent .285, so the baseline sits .840em down the line box and the digits' ink is centred .3675em above it — .0275em above the line box's own centre. So a shape declares --nf, the numeral's fraction of --sz, and the offset is --nf × .0275, computed where the font size is. There is no stored --nudge any more, and that is the point: the old arrangement stored the product, so every change of size silently invalidated six offsets — which is exactly how this went wrong.

Across, it was not centred, and the cause is a rule everyone knows and nobody pictures: letter-spacing is applied after every character, the last one included. The em's layout box is n·A + n·L wide around a run of ink n·A + (n−1)·L long — one whole tracking narrower than its own contents — so centring the box overhangs the ink to the right by the full |L| and lands the numeral |L|/2 = .0225em off. Measured at damage size: .3042px at the old size, .3861px at this one, the same on every value. margin-inline-end:.045em gives the box back exactly that column, and the two must always move together. What is left is the value's own asymmetry — tabular figures centre each glyph in one shared advance, so a number whose first digit is narrow and whose last is wide leans right by (wlast − wfirst)/4, about .8px on 10, nothing at all on 11. That is a property of what was rolled rather than of the box, and no rule in a stylesheet reaches it.

// THE PORTRAITWho rolled it, answered before you read anything

Endfield never drops a portrait into a panel as a picture — it drops it in as tone: one channel, bled off the edge, sitting under the type rather than beside it. The face is far faster than a name at answering whose roll is this, and here it costs no layout at all, because it lives behind type that was already there.

The first pass locked the blend by direction — screen on Hope so it could only lighten, multiply on Fear so it could only darken — so that it was impossible to break the text's contrast. True, and nearly invisible: the end of the field I put it on is the end that blend has no headroom at. A screen over pale gold has almost nowhere left to go.

So the constraint is stated directly instead. The image is mapped into a two-colour ramp whose endpoints both sit inside the band its ink needs, and every pixel is a blend of those two, so every pixel is in band by construction — no tuning, no per-image luck. Built as three layers, each bounded: the shadow colour, the image greyscaled and clamped by brightness(), the hue multiplied onto it so a Fear silhouette stays violet instead of going grey, then screened onto the shadow. Measured on the sample art, all three use their whole permitted range end to end.

Hope is the flat one, and it has to be. Its ink is the dark one, so the portrait's shadows can never go below 4.5:1 against near-black — the ramp bottoms out at #a8831f and spans 2.5×, where Fear's runs from near-black to mid-violet and spans 3.2×. That is a property of a dark-on-bright plate, not something to tune away.

// THE REDHow Endfield actually uses it

Red is never a colour there, it is a rung. Normal / Hard / Brutal: the top tier is not more red than the two below it, it is a saturated red field plus material — a bloom, embers, a decorated cap — that the lower tiers simply do not have. The I / II / III / IV threat plates do the same thing: the live one is lit and bracketed, the dormant ones are flat.

So the critical is red end to end now. It was gold-on-red on the argument that a critical counts as a roll with Hope — true as a rule, but as a picture it made the loudest thing on the card look like it was wearing another outcome's jewellery, and the eye read gold first. The Hope is still stated where it belongs: the card hands you a +1 Hope to take. The ramp does the work the gold was doing — --crit-dk in the shadow, --crit in the field, --crit-br at the edges where it is hottest.

The honest cost: red is already --wound on this sheet, the material of a Hit Point. This one is hotter, lighter and glows, and it never appears on a mark — but it is a real adjacency, so it is written down here rather than discovered in play.

// THE PLATEA, across every state it has

Full-bleed field, ghost word running off the frame, the total at 48px, alone, and with no underline through it. The field's luminance band is set by its own ink now, which is why Hope runs light → deep and Fear runs deep → light: one rule, and the near-white stop that used to put dark ink on #6d4a08 at 2.27:1 is gone.

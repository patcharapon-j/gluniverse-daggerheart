// TM · 0 · THE PROBLEMAn eleventh colour reads as an eleventh domain

Root and Void belong to one campaign frame rather than to the game, and they have to be tellable from the ten the books print. Hue alone cannot do it. Sweeping OKLCH inside the family's band (L 40–72, C .07–.15) for the point furthest from all ten, the best-separated cold point sits 0.091 from its nearest neighbour in OKLab — and the corebook's own tightest genuine pair, Codex and Midnight, is 0.093. The best hue available is worse than the closest call the books already make.

Which frees the hues to be the factions' own — Rootwoken is earth and growth, Starbound is silver and platinum — and moves the whole question onto the frame. Below is one option per card constant, each on its own, so they can be judged separately and then stacked. The card layout does not move in any of them. Text is placeholder; this study is about the object.

// TM · A · THE TRIMGold is Hope. These decks are not bought with Hope.

One line · --trim

Gold appears on every card the game prints, in five places at once: the hairline under both corner blocks, the recall bolt, the seam, the type badge and the tail of the bottom bar. It is the most repeated constant the component has, and the only one carried by every card regardless of domain, level, artwork or type. It is also the one with a meaning — gold on a card is Hope.

Five separate pieces of furniture change at once, so it reads instantly and at any size.

Silver against gold is the oldest print distinction there is, and it is one declaration.

Both decks share one metal, so the trim says "campaign", not "which".

// TM · B · THE DOUBLE CUTChange the silhouette

One line · clip-path

Every card in the system carries exactly one 15px chamfer, bottom-right. Cut the bottom-left as well and the outline is symmetrical, which no corebook card is.

Shape survives everything — thumbnail size, a peek, artwork, colourblindness, a face-down card.

Costs no ink at all, so it composes with every other option and with any art.

Quiet on its own. At 200px it is a decision you have to already know about to notice.

// TM · C · INVERTED STOCKThe one card in the hand that is the other colour

Nine tokens · --paper --tint --ink* --line*

The first draft of this said "print them on black board" — which is what a corebook card is already printed on, since the default theme is dark. It came out two RGB points from its neighbours. The constant is not dark, it is the same substrate as everything else, so a marked card takes the other theme's paper: ivory on a dark sheet, board on a light one. Flip the theme toggle in the bar and they swap with it.

Unmistakable at any size, in any hand, next to any card, in either theme.

Loud. Two of these among nine become the thing the loadout is about.

The silver trim nearly disappears on ivory, so A and C fight each other — see G.

// TM · D · THE EDGEBounded, where the paper otherwise just stops

One rule · .card::after

No corebook card has an edge of any kind — they are a picture, a panel and a bar, and the paper simply stops. This one is bounded: a hairline of the set's metal round the outside, a dark line under it so the metal has something to sit on, and the domain's own light bleeding inward off the rim. Three declarations, three jobs — the edge, the shade under it, and the glow.

The glow is the same light the motif below is lit by, so the rim and the field read as one effect rather than two.

inset:0, so the chamfer cuts it — a clip-path clips pseudo-elements too. The 15px diagonal carries only the glow, which is right: the cut corner is the family mark and a line across it would argue with it.

Spends the card's one free pseudo-element, which is also what a foil overlay would want.

// TM · E · THE MARQUEThe footer names the box it came out of

One rule + content · foot

The footer's left cell prints the domain name on every card — the fifth statement of a fact the sigil, the corner blocks, the bar and the type badge have already made. On a marked card it prints the frame's name instead.

Explicit. Nobody has to infer anything, and it survives being described out loud.

Costs nothing: the cell already existed and was saying something redundant.

2.5cqw mono. Legible on a posted card, gone in a loadout row.

// TM · M · THE MOTIFCrystal and roots, in the panel, lit from below

Two rules + two SVGs · .body + .body::before

It is in the panel and never on the plate, which is the whole reason this version of the idea survives and the last one did not. A pattern over the artwork competes with a painting somebody commissioned; a pattern in the panel is the card's own stationery, and the panel is the one region of a Daggerheart card that is flat colour by design. Real art can land tomorrow and nothing here moves.

The drawings are generated, by tools/make-marked-motifs.mjs, and both are arithmetic a person is bad at: a honeycomb has to be a real offset lattice (dx = √3·r, dy = 1.5·r, alternate rows shifted half a cell) or it reads as chicken wire, and a vine has to taper, which is a polygon offset along a curve's own normals. The first hand-plotted pair came out as a barcode and a field of grass. The seed is fixed, so the committed SVGs are reproducible — run it twice and git sees nothing.

Four layers. The ground — three radials in the panel's background: a wide bloom off the bottom edge, a tighter core inside it, and one off-centre so the light has a direction. The bloom — the motif again, blurred, added as light; this is the whole of why the figure reads as luminous rather than as a stencil, because line art glows when a soft copy sits under the crisp one. The motif, crisp, over its own bloom. The falloff, a second mask layer intersected with the drawing.

Sized off the panel's width and anchored to its bottom, so it holds proportion when fit() compacts a card and never grows into the prose on a sparse one.

Says which deck — the one thing the trim deliberately does not.

The blur is in cqw, not px, or a 198px loadout card gets a 300px card's blur radius.

The only option that costs assets and a tool rather than a declaration — and one blur filter per card, which is nothing on a sheet and two hundred in the compendium browser.

The motif starts 51.5% down the panel and a one-paragraph card's prose stops at 38.6%, so on most cards they never meet. A grimoire is the case that tests it — three feature runs fill the panel to the footer, and the question is whether rules text stays readable over the drawing. The falloff over the bottom fifth is there for the footer's own micro-type, which is --ink-3 at 2.5cqw and was sitting exactly where the light was brightest: 2.75:1, against 4.43 on plain paper.

// TM · F · THE PICKAll five

Recommended

Signals at four distances, none of which fights artwork. The edge and the trim carry it across the room, the cut carries it into a thumbnail and a face-down card, the motif says which deck once the card is open, and the marque names it outright when you are close enough to read prose. The layout, the stock and the type stay the game's, so the card still belongs to the deck it is sitting in.

Nothing here needs artwork to cooperate, so it all holds when real art lands.

// TM · G · WITH INVERTED STOCKThe same pick, if loud is what you want

Same three, plus the paper. Worth seeing beside F before choosing — this is the version people notice from across the table, and the version that makes two cards in a nine-card loadout the loudest things on the sheet. Note what happens to the trim: silver on ivory is nearly gone, so stacking A on C spends A for nothing. If C wins, the trim wants to go dark rather than metal.

// reference · gluniverse-daggerheart

A system built from
light, weight and
derived numbers.

This is the design study for a Daggerheart implementation on Foundry VTT v14. It documents a visual language taken from Arknights: Endfield — its typographic discipline, its use of saturated fields as tiers rather than decoration, its habit of breaking panel boundaries — and pulled back from the sci-fi surface into something genre-neutral, because Daggerheart is not only a fantasy game.

It is written for another agent. Everything below is a rule with a reason attached, and every specimen is rendered with the production technique rather than described. Where a number appears, it was derived or measured; none of them are taste. The last section is the working method — read it before changing anything.

// 01 · originWhat was taken from Endfield, and what was left behind

Endfield's interface is loud, but it is not loud everywhere at once. Studying the screenshots, five moves do nearly all the work — and only one of them is science fiction.

Taken · red is a rung, not a colourNormal / Hard / Brutal: the top tier is not more red, it is a saturated red field plus material — a bloom, embers, a decorated cap — that the tiers below simply do not have. Same with the I/II/III/IV threat plates: the live one is lit and bracketed, the dormant ones are flat.
Taken · the ghost wordA copy of the headline at 3× running off the frame at 3–15% alpha. The cheapest way to make a small box feel like it was cropped out of something larger. On every hero panel Endfield ships.
Taken · portrait as toneEndfield never drops a portrait in as a picture. It goes in as one channel, bled off the edge, under the type rather than beside it — so it costs no layout at all.
Taken · the mono eyebrow// EVENT CENTER, wide-tracked uppercase mono over everything. It is the system's connective tissue and it is what makes an otherwise plain panel read as instrumentation.
Taken · broken boundariesObjects sitting across a panel edge — half on the colour, half off it. The single move that most stops a rectangle looking like a rectangle.
Left behind · the sci-fi surfaceNo hexagons, no reticles, no fake telemetry, no Chinese-industrial hazard stripes, no glitch. Those are Endfield's fiction, not its craft. Daggerheart has to survive a pirate campaign and a cyberpunk one in the same stylesheet.
the substitution

Where Endfield uses yellow-black industrial as its accent, this system uses Daggerheart's own two currencies — gold for Hope, violet for Fear. That single swap does most of the de-sci-fi-ing on its own, because the palette stops citing a factory and starts citing the game.

// 02 · the substrateThe palette is a set of roles, not a set of colours

Dark is the base theme and light is a class on <html>. That is a change of substrate, not a repaint — so the palette is split into roles first. Every value names what it is for, and each theme answers the same question its own way.

substrate law

The dark theme is cool and desaturated, not neutral grey. At a true neutral #1a1a1a the gold reads olive. A warm gold and a violet both have to sit on this substrate without it arguing with either, and only a cool base manages that.

frame law

The frame does not flip. Window chrome and the tab strip are dark in both themes because they are the Foundry app, not the character sheet. Everything you read a value off is paper; everything that holds it is frame. Two exceptions, both about depth rather than data: the portrait diorama and the Evasion/Armor silhouettes.

Fill versus text — every accent is a pair

A saturated hue that works as a fill is almost never legible as text, and the two diverge differently per theme. So each accent ships twice.

// 03 · colour lawFour hues, and each one means exactly one thing

The system runs on a near-neutral field with a very small number of hues, and each is spent on a single meaning. Introducing a fifth hue is the most expensive change anyone can make here.

HueMeansMay appear onMay never
GoldHope the Hope gem, the Hope field, a spent-Hope term, the level plate, gold coins, the seam rule be a generic "highlight" or a hover state
VioletFear the Fear die, the Fear field, a term the GM paid Fear for appear on the player's side as decoration
Redharm — and, one rung up, a critical Hit Point marks, damage dice, the damage plate, the critical's material mark anything that is not harm
Cold blueStress the Stress mark only anything else at all

Cold blue is not an aesthetic choice. It is the only quadrant of the wheel left once gold is Hope, violet is Fear and red is a wound — and choosing it means a Stress mark turning violet at maximum is unmissable.

Red is a rung

Directly from Endfield. A critical is not "the same card, brighter" — it is a red field plus four materials nothing below it may have: embers, a corner bracket, a lit glass edge, and a struck badge.

critical fielddeep → hot, so white ink starts on the safe end
the bracket 3.81:1 on the field. The true hot red managed 1.94 and vanished — a frame you cannot see is not a frame
the badgenear-black on it at 7.06:1 — the brightest, highest-contrast object anywhere in the system, which is exactly the job
the wound what red already means: one Hit Point
hope fieldlight → deep, because Hope's ink is the dark one
fear fielddeep → light, same rule, opposite ink
gradient law

A field's luminance band is set by its own ink, never by taste. It always starts at the end its ink is safest on. Hope's ink is dark so the field may go as light as it likes and bottoms out at #a38122 (4.92:1); Fear's ink is white so the field tops out at --o itself (5.64:1); critical the same (4.98:1). One rule, two directions.

the honest cost, recorded not hidden

Red is already --wound, the material of a Hit Point, and the critical is also red. The critical's red is hotter, lighter and glows, and it never appears on a mark — but it is a real adjacency. It is written down here rather than discovered in play. Anything that widens this adjacency is a regression.

The gold that was removed

The critical used to be gold-on-red, on the argument that a critical counts as a roll with Hope. True as a rule, and wrong as a picture: it made the loudest object on the card look like it was wearing another outcome's jewellery, and the eye went to the gold first. The Hope is still stated — in the +1 Hope the card hands you, which is where the rule actually lives.

// 04 · typographyThree faces, and a strict division of labour

FaceJobTypical setting
Google Sans
--f-display		 Numbers and verdicts. Anything that is the answer to the question the component exists to answer totals at 48px/.82, -.055em, tabular; verdicts at 15px uppercase
Google Sans Text
--f-ui		 Prose. Card rules text, descriptions, anything read as a sentence 12–13.5px, line-height 1.55–1.68
Google Sans Code
--f-mono		 Labels, eyebrows, arithmetic. Everything instrumental — the connective tissue borrowed from Endfield 7.5–11px, 700, uppercase, tracked .13–.24em
tracking law

Mono tracking encodes rank, not decoration. A name is a caption and wants air at .22em; a label is a sentence and does not, at .15em. Section eyebrows sit widest at .24em. Getting this wrong reads immediately as amateur, and the fix is never "pick a nicer number" — it is deciding what rank the run holds.

Weight is bought with stroke, not with a heavier cut

Google Sans ships 400–700, and the display numerals already start at 700. Extra mass comes from -webkit-text-stroke: 1.2px on a 48px total is about a half-step of weight — enough that the number stops being "large text" and starts being the object the card is about. The die's numeral uses .016 × --sz, which is the same half-step made scale-invariant.

markup trap

The total is marked up as a <u> so the three parts of its row name themselves without a class each — and it was drawing its own underline through the one glyph on the card nobody should have to read past. Every semantic element used for structure here carries an explicit text-decoration and an explicit color: Foundry ships an h3{color:…} rule that beats inheritance.

// 05 · geometrySilhouette carries meaning before colour does

The cut corner is the family mark

One 45° chamfer on the bottom-right, on everything the player owns. It scales with the object's importance, and it is the single strongest identity signal in the system — which is why removing it is how the GM's card is othered.

The Foundry window takes 14px, the mark boxes 5px, armour slots take a second chamfer on the opposite corner so the row reads as the shield's at a glance.

Three silhouettes, and only three

A diamond is Hope and Fear and nothing else. A square is a box you mark, and a coin is the only circle besides the Proficiency dots. A die you roll and a pip you fill are two different objects and it was worth two different shapes.

Struck, not lit. The gem is a light and carries no gradient; a coin is metal, so it gets a rim, an inner ring and a specular arc. That contrast is how you know at a glance that one is a resource and the other is a mood.

A die is drawn as the solid, seen down a face normal

Not as one face of it. That is the shape a reader already has in their hand, and it is why a d12 and a d20 are told apart on a table at a glance — a dodecahedron is round, a d20 is angular, long before anyone counts anything.

DieSilhouetteFront faceFacetsFront-face RSilhouette RRatio

Both ratios are 0.618 = φ−1, the golden section that builds both solids. The projections were computed from the actual vertex coordinates — icosahedron down a 3-fold axis, dodecahedron down a 5-fold axis — not eyeballed.

the correction worth keeping

The d12 was drawn as a stylised pentagon for a while, on the argument that a decagon at 38px is a coin. It was — but the fault was the shading, not the silhouette. With a flat ring and no lit edges, any ten-sided outline is a disc. Faceted properly it reads as the solid, and the silhouette differing from the front face is exactly what makes it read as a solid rather than as one face seen straight on.

Everything else stays a square. A d8 face and a d10 face are shapes nobody recognises at 26px, and a reader squinting at a silhouette to learn something the arithmetic line already states in words is doing the harder thing. The d20 is the exception because in this game it is rolled exactly once, by the GM, at you — it never competes for meaning, so its shape is not asking you to tell two things apart. It is telling you which side of the table the card came from.

The numeral's offset is derived, and scale-invariant

Every shape is a regular polygon concentric with its box, so its area centroid is simply 50%. What remains is the font's own offset: Google Sans has ascent .965 and descent .285, so at line-height 1 the baseline sits .840em down the line box and the digits' ink is centred .3675em above it — putting ink centre at 48.79% of the die. 50 − 48.79 = 1.21%, hence --nudge:.0121. It is scale-invariant because the font size is a fraction of --sz.

// 06 · material & lightObjects are lit, not merely coloured

This is where the "premium" reads from. Nothing in the system is a flat rectangle with a border; every object states what it is made of and where the light is.

The gem construction

Used by every die and every Hope pip: a flat saturated front face, a brighter rim where the light leaves the edge, and a bloom on a wrapper element — the wrapper matters, because a filter applied to the clipped shape itself is clipped away by the very shape it is describing.

The facet ring

A conic-gradient with hard stops on the facet boundaries, plus a lit edge at each boundary. Sector values are cos of the angle to a light at the upper left, mapped to 6–26% black.

  • The floor is 6, not 0, so the front face's own outline never disappears into the facet beside it on the lit side.
  • The ceiling is 26 because that is where the gold d12's dark numeral still measures 4.8:1 on its deepest facet — and it is the dark-ink dice, not the white-ink ones, that a darker ring costs.
  • The lit edge is a 5° wedge, not a stroke: 2px at the outer rim and 1.2px at the inner one, so it never falls below the rendering floor at any radius. It is painted in exactly --facec, because a real edge catches the light.
the only-darkens guarantee

The ring may only ever darken. That is correct lighting — the front face is square to you and catches the most light — and it is also a contract: no numeral ever sits on anything lighter than the face colour it was measured against, so every contrast figure in this system survives the facets. The numeral is painted on the die, not fitted inside one plane of it — "20" on a triangle overhangs onto the upper facets, which is precisely why the guarantee has to hold.

rendering floor

A 1px line at these sizes is a sub-pixel line at partial alpha — it does not render as a thin line, it renders as a grey smudge. Anything that must read as a hard mark is expressed as a proportion with a max() floor: max(1px, --sz × .028). This has bitten the facet edge, the strike-out X and the die rim.

The strike — one meaning, nothing else may borrow it

Unlit was not enough. The losing duality die is unlit and it still counts — both duality dice are summed. A helper's d6 that did not win is thrown away entirely. Two different fates were wearing the same face.

the X means one thing

This did not count. Nothing else on any card may use it. It is --xmark-p, the material already used for a struck-out scar, and it carries a halo in the face's own colour — because --xmark-p and --ink-2 are one step apart in value, and without the halo the strike and the numeral merge into a single grey blob.

A repeating 114° stripe at 3–6% white laid over every coloured field. It is barely visible and it is doing real work: it stops a large flat gradient reading as a swatch. On a critical it warms to rgba(255,216,203,.22) and flips to dark stripes in the light theme.

// 07 · the portraitA duotone whose endpoints are both in band by construction

The first version locked the blend by direction — screen on Hope so it could only lighten, multiply on Fear so it could only darken — on the argument that this made breaking the text's contrast impossible. That was true, and it made the portrait nearly invisible: the end of the field it sits on is the end its own blend has no headroom at.

state the constraint, do not approximate it

The image is mapped into a two-colour ramp whose endpoints both sit inside the band its ink needs. Every pixel is a blend of those two, so every pixel is in band by construction — no tuning, no per-image luck, and it holds for any art a user drops in.

Four layers, each bounded:

  • .por — the shadow colour, and the mask that fades it off the edge
  • u — the image, greyscaled and clamped to --pk by brightness(). contrast(1.32) runs before it, pushing more of the image to the two ends of the ramp, which is what makes a narrow band still read as a figure
  • b — the hue, multiplied on, so a Fear silhouette stays violet instead of going grey
  • i — screens the pair onto the shadow: A + k·T·(1−A)
FieldShadow → inkLight → ink--pkSpan
hope#a8831f · 5.12:1#ecd889 · 12.68:1 .782.5×
fear#150929 · 19.04:1#6f5b94 · 5.86:1 .503.2×
critical#3a0705 · 17.32:1#955247 · 5.88:1 .462.9×

Hope is the flat one, and it has to be. Its ink is the dark one, so the portrait's shadows can never go below 4.5:1 against near-black. That is a property of a dark-on-bright plate, not something to tune away.

the bound must include everything laid over it

--pk on the hostile field is .44, not the .50 it started at — because the bound has to include the shard stripe, which lays 6% white over whatever is under it. At .50 the portrait's brightest pixel plus a stripe took white ink to 4.31:1. Below .44 the portrait stops being the binding constraint at all and the field's own light end takes over at 4.79.

The mask is a plain fade to nothing — no edge, no vignette, no hairline. Anything drawn at the boundary reads as a seam, and a seam is the opposite of what "faded at the edge" asked for.

the token-flip trap

A rendered surface — a coloured field, a damage bar, the GM card — does not flip with the sheet's substrate. Any theme-reactive token used inside one will silently invert and destroy its own contrast in one of the two themes. Either use fixed hexes, or re-declare the role tokens locally so everything downstream resolves against the right substrate. This trap has been sprung three separate times.

// 09 · motionEvery animation is an event, and different events move differently

motion law

Motion states what happened. Three marks that are three different events get three different animations, not one animation in three colours. If two things move the same way, a reader is entitled to assume they are the same kind of thing.

EventMotionTiming
card arrival7px drop with a light sweep raking across behind it .32s (.2,.85,.3,1) + .64s sweep
dice landingovershoot to 1.14 and settle .4s (.16,1.06,.3,1), .06s stagger
verdictwipe in from the left via clip-path:inset() .36s, +.06s
totalrises 8px from 94% scale.4s, +.04s
a Hit Pointthe gash lands whole from 1.5× — you do not watch a wound being drawn — then bleeds. Two arms, staggered, because it tore twice 160ms strike, 340ms bleed, arm 2 at +70ms
a Stressscraped in along its own axis under load, cold and thin; the recess flickers the way something does when it is scored rather than struck—
an Armor slotboth arms land together — one impact, not two strokes — with a gold flash and a hard square ring, and the box's corners break away where the mark exits —
critical sealstruck, from 2.2× with a −9° rotation, after everything else has settled.42s (.2,1.5,.4,1), delay .6s
stillness law

The box does not move. It used to compress and overshoot on every mark — the gem's grammar borrowed wholesale, and wrong here, because Hope arrives one at a time and Hit Points arrive three at a time. Seven boxes bouncing is a row of jelly. The recess flushes, the ring flashes, and the box is still. All the motion belongs to the mark.

Staggers must stop accumulating. A critical at Proficiency 6 lands twelve dice; past the sixth the delay is capped, or the last die arrives most of a second after the number it belongs to.

motion is not optional

Nothing here honours prefers-reduced-motion. It used to: every animation was disabled wholesale under @media (prefers-reduced-motion:reduce), ambient ones included, with the opacity-dimming states restored to full. That was thirty-odd blocks across sixteen stylesheets and four modules, each one a second definition of a component's resting state that nothing on a study page could show was wrong — and every one of them had to be remembered again on the next animation added. The motion in this system is the system saying what just happened: the wound landing, the card travelling, the reel stopping on a total. So it plays, always, on every client. A table that wants less of it turns it down in the operating system's own compositor rather than in ours.

// 10 · the cardAnatomy, and what a card is not allowed to do

A chat card is a fixed 300px in Foundry and does not scale. Study stages are therefore fixed-width too — a stage that stretches is showing a component that does not exist.

PartHoldsRule
the fieldfull-bleed colour, the eyebrow, the verdict, the total luminance band set by its own ink
the eyebrowwho rolled it · what they rolled a flex row, not one truncating line. The name is flex:none — it is the answer to "whose roll is this" and is never what gets given up. The label shrinks and ellipses
the totalthe number 48px, alone, no underline through it, on its own row
the ghostthe outcome again at 54px, running off the frame 3–15% alpha, pointer-events:none, user-select:none
the stripthe dice, and the arithmetic pushed right the audit trail — the only place the card shows its work. Capped at 54% width
the meta linewhat kind of roll · versus what 8px mono, the smallest type on the card and the one place with no contrast margin
the actionswhat you may do next chamfered buttons; a button that is not yours is outlined, not filled
silence law

A card does not narrate its own ignorance. Most duality rolls are made without a Difficulty, and the card simply says WITH HOPE — which is the whole truth of that roll. There is no "no difficulty" chip and no placeholder about waiting. A missing fact is not a fact worth a slot, and a card announcing what it does not know is worse than a card that is quiet. On a reaction roll, where there is no Hope-or-Fear sentence to fall back on, the sentence is omitted entirely and the number stands alone.

authority law

A card is authoritative about its own number and says nothing about anyone else's state. A damage card reports a quantity; how many Hit Points that becomes depends on thresholds, armour and resistance that live on the target, not here.

The five arrangements, and why A won

Five layouts were built to the same data and kept side by side rather than described. B–E are frozen in the repository as the record of the choice — nothing about them is being proposed; they are there so the reason A won stays legible.

  • B · Split — the number is housed in its own saturated field. Reads bigger at a smaller size. Endfield's Total Test Criteria · 45 block.
  • C · Wedge — the field's lower edge is a hard diagonal and the dice sit across it. The strongest anti-rectangle move; costs the most height.
  • D · Black bar — colour reduced to a lit lower edge. Quietest by a mile, and quiet is what buys the critical its shock. The only one that does not fight a domain card for attention.
  • E · Stack — the verdict broken across two lines at display weight. Fastest to read; worst with a long name, and the only variant with no room for a seal.
  • A · Plate — full-bleed field, ghost running off the frame, the total alone at 48. Won on having the most room to absorb the cases the card has to survive rather than show off in: a long name, an actor with no art, a critical at Proficiency 6.

// 11 · the GMOthering, and the one mark that carries the outcome

The GM is a force, not a participant. Two candidates were built: the player card wearing a hostile red field, and a deliberately othered object. Both parsed cleanly in isolation. The second won, and the reason only appeared in a log — a real chat sequence — where the first read as one more participant at the table, which is the one thing the GM is not.

  • No cut corner. That notch is the family mark on every player card in this system, so its absence says "not one of yours" before any hue does.
  • No portrait, no field. Near-black throughout, one lit rail down the left. The number drops two sizes.
  • The verdict is set in mono, not display. The player cards say their outcome in a voice; this one reports it.
  • The rail carries the outcome — red on a hit, cold steel on a miss, hot on a critical. The card has exactly one mark, so the mark does the work. It is 4px, not 3: at 3 it read as a border, and this is a graphic carrying meaning.
two lit states, one unlit

A lit rail means a hit is being claimed. A withheld Difficulty claims nothing, so it is unlit alongside the miss. An earlier version computed hit = total >= (dc ?? 0), which collapsed unknown into true and painted a red rail on a card that had said nothing. Unknown is a third value and must be modelled as one.

the collision only a log exposes

The GM numeral used to be the wound highlight — which is the damage bar's numeral colour, and a damage card lands one message after this one. Two different objects back to back in the same clothes took back the very thing the othering was bought to do. The numeral is --ink now. Red means a quantity of harm and nothing else; a d20 against Evasion is a comparison.

// 12 · methodHow to extend this without degrading it

These are the rules the system was actually built under. They matter more than any individual value on this page, because they are what produced the values.

01 · derive, do not eyeball

If a number can be computed, compute it. Die geometry from vertex coordinates. Facet values from the cosine of the angle to the light. The numeral's offset from the font's ascent and descent metrics. Edge widths from the sub-pixel rendering floor at both radii. A number chosen by eye is a number nobody can defend later — and, in practice, it is the one that turns out to be wrong.

02 · verify by measurement, not by looking

Screenshot it, then probe it: walk to the first opaque background and compute the real contrast in both themes; re-derive each total from its own printed terms; detect clipping and escapes; sample after the animation has landed to prove every die is inside its own range. Bugs found this way include a die that never landed, an arithmetic line that did not match its own total, and a study page's own CSS leaking into the component it was displaying.

03 · judge in context, never in isolation

Both GM candidates were fine alone. The log is what exposed the damage-bar collision. Any new card must be reviewed in a real sequence beside the cards it will actually appear next to.

04 · freeze the losers

Rejected variants stay in the repository, minus whatever was specific to the winner, and pick up shared improvements for free. They are not proposals — they are the record of the choice, so the reason the winner won stays legible to whoever arrives next.

05 · write the cost down

Where a decision has a real downside — red doing two jobs, E having no seal, the strike covering its own numeral — it is stated in a comment at the point of the decision. A cost recorded is a cost the next person can weigh. A cost discovered in play is a bug.

06 · one hue, one meaning

Before adding a colour, establish that no existing one can carry the job and that the new one will never appear anywhere else. The system's legibility comes from the smallness of the palette, and every addition spends that down.

07 · scope every selector

Class names here are short and generic — .rc, .nm, .tb, .p. Unscoped they reach into Foundry's own chrome the moment this ships. Short names are worth keeping; global ones are not. State color and text-decoration explicitly on every semantic element, because Foundry has host rules that beat inheritance.

08 · the study page is not the component

Twice, a study page's own CSS matched inside a card it was displaying and repainted it. Use direct-child combinators for page chrome (.vs .cards > s, never .vs .cards s), and never let a stage stretch a component past the width it will really have.

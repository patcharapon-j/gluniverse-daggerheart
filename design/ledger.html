The change log

Everything else this system posts is an event somebody chose: a roll, a card shown, a rest taken. This is the opposite — the record of what happened to a sheet while nobody was posting anything. A player marks three Stress and the GM, looking at the map, has no idea; a card comes out of the vault and the only witness is the sheet it happened on. The rules ask a table to keep these numbers where everyone can see them, and until now the only place any of them existed was the sheet of the person holding it.

// THE UNITNet, not keystrokes

The unit is the change, not the write. Applying damage is four writes — armour, stress, hope, hit points — and one event. Fixing a miscount is three writes in two seconds and one correction. So entries coalesce and what lands is the net: 3 → 5 Hit Points, never +1, +1, −1, +2. Which also means a net of zero posts nothing, and taking back a box you marked by mistake is not something the table has to watch happen.

one hit four writes, one card

This is a Severe hit with an Armor Slot spent. Four separate writes to the document, inside about fifty milliseconds, and one thing that happened. The card is the event, not the transaction log.

a short rest's worth read at a glance

A run of dim boxes ending in rings is a heal. Ending in bright ones it is a hit. That is the whole reading, and it happens before you get to the numerals.

// THE STRIPA delta with no total is half a fact

“Marked 2 Stress” does not say whether she is one box from Vulnerable, which is the only thing anybody wanted to know. So the row carries the track as it now stands and says which part of it just moved — settled history recedes, the part that moved does not. That is the damage dialog's rule, in the past tense.

the three states on, up, dn

.on is context and is deliberately quiet. .up is what this entry marked. .dn is what it gave back, drawn as the ring it left rather than as a dimmer grey — an empty box and a box that just emptied are the two things this row exists to tell apart, and a shade between two greys does not.

one box size per message, not per row

Twelve Hit Points over six Stress. Sized independently that is two different boxes in one glance, which reads as two different kinds of thing. They are not — they are the same box, and the shorter row simply ends early. MARKS made this argument first.

// THE OTHER SHAPESHope is gems, a card's pool is chits

Neither is redrawn here. Hope is gems everywhere in this system and a card's counters are the row the loadout draws — the log is not the place to introduce a second drawing of either. What does change is that nothing is pressable: a control in a record would be offering to change a number that changed three hours ago.

hope and a scarred slot

A scarred slot is not an empty one. The ceiling is four and the row is six wide — two lit, two dark, two dead. Drawing four would quietly drop the permanent half of what happened to this character; drawing six lit sockets would promise Hope she cannot hold.

a card's counters and a card crossing

A move has no quantity, so it takes no numeral. A name and a direction, with the domain as a pip — the one hue on this card that is not a track's material, and right here for the reason it is right everywhere: the colour is how you know which card.

// STORAGEWhy none of this is an svg

Foundry's sanitiser strips every <svg> out of stored message content. A posted card survives that by being redrawn from a flag on render. A mark track cannot: a marked box that loses its X does not look broken, it looks unmarked, which is a lie rather than a degradation. So this is not the sheet's MARKS row — every mark here is CSS on an empty element, GEMS and CHITS were already <i> and <b>, and the whole message survives storage exactly as posted. No flag, no redraw, no render hook.

the same card, unstyled what a foreign client sees

Truthful, and readable. Stylesheet off, markup intact: every number, every name and every verb is still there and still correct. That is the whole argument for drawing the boxes rather than borrowing them.

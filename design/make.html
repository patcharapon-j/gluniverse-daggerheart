The rail carries both, and that is the entire layout argument. Choose a class and Evasion and Hit Points land under your cursor — the step you are on and the number it moves are inches apart. Put the steps in a tab strip along the top and that relationship is gone, and with it the reason any of this is live. The numerals are the book's step numbers, which is why they read 1, 2, 3, 5, 7, 8: step 4 is the panel beneath them, and 6 and 9 are prose that belongs on the bio tab.

THE CLASS ROWThe largest decision in the window gets a page, not a tile

Nine classes, one per row. As a 268px option a class was two domains, a name, two numbers and a sentence all competing for the same column — which is a fair description of what a class is not. It fixes your domains, your Evasion, your Hit Points and half your card pool, and you make it once. So the row splits by kind: the class mark on a plate tinted by its domain pair on the left, everything written on the right. Domain sigils, class abilities and the Hope ability are all present in the row; classes have no hover card. There is no flavour paragraph: this is a choice between nine sets of rules, and every word in the row is something the class does. Identity is a header rather than a column — it is five short facts that never grow, and the rules run from three lines to fifteen, so a column beside them stood empty for the height of a Beastform. The rules take the full width in a columns block, which balances and never stretches a short plate to a tall one's height the way a two-track grid does. Press a class and it opens downward into its own subclass drawer: the cards were a grid of their own below the list under a heading naming the class they belonged to, and adjacency says that better than a caption does. The panel is a container and the press is the row inside it, because a subclass card is a button and a button inside a button is not markup a browser will keep.

THE CARD GRIDThree steps choose printed cards, so they draw printed cards

A subclass, an ancestry and a domain card are objects that exist on paper, and a text summary of one lies by omission about the two things a card carries structurally rather than in prose: the domain — which is the hue and the corner sigils — and the level and Recall Cost, which are the corner blocks. .card is container-query driven, so the component that draws a 300px chat plate draws a 176px grid cell unchanged. fit() sizes every card independently at both widths, exactly as it does on the character sheet and in chat. The builder does not add a grid-wide image or type scale.

THE EQUIPMENT TABLEChapter 2 is a table, and this is the one step that is too

Every other step offers something you can hold a picture of, and a grid is right for those because the thing you compare is the whole object. Equipment is not that. A longsword is one line of chapter 2, and what you compare across thirty-five of them is five columns of the same five facts — trait, range, damage, burden, feature. A tile states each fact in a different place on every tile, so comparing two weapons' damage means finding the damage twice; a column is read down. It is also the only list here that runs long enough for it to matter: unrestricted turns this step into two hundred and four primaries. The groups are the book's own tables — primaries print as Physical and Magic per tier, because a magic weapon rolls with a Spellcast trait you may not have. Secondaries and armour are one table each and get no caption, since inventing one would claim a division the book does not make. Every row keeps data-pk, so hovering still opens the printed card — the tile was a summary of a card that already exists in the peek layer, and the table is a better summary of the same card. subgrid is what lines the columns up across groups: one grid per group and Physical and Magic each solve their own widths, which is two tables of the same five facts aligned differently.

DEAD, NOT HIDDENAn option you may not take says why on itself

dlg.css established this for the trait picker, where the greyed row is one of six you can see the rest of. It does more work here. Filtering the illegal cards out of a domain deck leaves a deck that looks complete — the player never learns the rule exists, and the first time they wonder why a Wizard cannot take Whirlwind there is nothing on screen that ever mentioned it. Present and captioned is the rule being taught.

THE SPREADA budget you can see the shape of

Six chips, placed by drag or by click, consumed as they land. Overspending is not something this control refuses — it is something it cannot express, which is the difference between a form that validates and a form that cannot be wrong. A spent chip keeps its slot as a hole: the budget is six objects and its shape is the information, and a row that reflows on every placement moves the chip you were about to click. Deliberately not pre-placed. prep.js opens roll-ready and is right to, because you meet it twice a minute; this is met once and its answer is permanent, and a default on a permanent choice is a default that gets accepted.

THE PLATEWhere you press, before and after

It takes height rather than overlaying, which is edit mode's rule and the same reasoning: a hint that lasts a second may float over the sheet, a state that lasts a session may not. And it does not vanish when it is finished — a control that disappears the moment you complete something is a control you will hunt for in three weeks when you want to swap one domain card.
